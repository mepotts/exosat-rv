An independent reproduction of the radial-velocity companion of CD-35 2722 B from archival CRIRES+ data, and first radial-velocity limits on η Tel B

Matthew Potts · independent analysis

11 August 2026 · figure-by-figure appendix added 12 August 2026 · reproduces Hoy et al. (2026), Nature · all data from the public ESO archive

Abstract

Hoy et al. (2026) report the radial-velocity detection of a ~0.9 MJup companion to the young brown dwarf CD-35 2722 B, with tentative evidence for a second, smaller companion. Starting from the raw CRIRES+ frames in the ESO archive and an independently configured extraction (cr2res 1.6.10 + viper), we reproduce the primary detection blind: a period search with no reference to the published values finds P ≈ 169–171 d as the top peak (ΔBIC +40), and the detection survives a barycentric-correction nuisance term (ΔBIC +25 to +28) despite the strong phase–BERV degeneracy of the epoch sampling. Three further tests place it: a permutation null on the true cadence gives a false-alarm probability of 5×10−4; all seventeen leave-one-out subsets return the same period; and the signal is absent from every other companion series reduced identically here, including η Tel B on the same instrument setting over a longer baseline — so the period is a property of the object, not of the observing calendar. Our per-epoch precision reaches 70–90 m s−1 against the published 57.7, and our semi-amplitude runs high by +19 to +34% (regression slope) or +39 to +54% (direct fit) — a hint, entangled with the same degeneracy, that we do not claim. The second companion does not reproduce: on the published Nature RV table itself, nested-sampling model comparison disfavours the two-companion model in every prior family tried, and the run-to-run reproducibility of such evidence integrals is itself several times larger than the uncertainty samplers report internally. Applying the validated pipeline to η Tel B — a brown dwarf with no previous RV measurements — yields a null with injection-calibrated 90% limits of m sin i ≳ 0.5–1.2 MJup for companions with P = 20–300 d, the first such constraint on the object.

1Introduction

Companion-side radial velocities — measuring the reflex motion of a directly imaged brown dwarf or giant planet from its own spectrum — were proposed by Vanderburg et al. (2018) and forecast to be feasible with CRIRES+ by Ruffio et al. (2022, in the framework of Lazzoni et al.). Three published searches returned nulls before Hoy et al. (2026, hereafter H26) reported the first detection: a P = 171.1 d, K ≈ 306 m s−1 signal in the L0-type companion CD-35 2722 B (31–37 MJup, AB Dor moving group), interpreted as a 0.92 MJup satellite, with tentative evidence (ΔlnZ = +2.62) for a second companion at 87.3 d.

A detection of this kind is worth reproducing end to end: the claimed amplitude sits far below the per-spectrum noise, the epoch sampling entangles the orbital phase with the barycentric correction (§4), and the published evidence for the second companion moved substantially between the preprint and the accepted version. This draft reports (i) a from-raw, independently configured reproduction of the H26 measurement (§2–4, Figs. 1–3), (ii) a re-examination of the two-companion evidence on the published RV table (§5, Table 3), and (iii) the first application of the validated pipeline to a new target, η Tel B (§6, Fig. 4). Every stage is validated by signal injection rather than by internal fit statistics; the distinction turns out to matter (§7). §8 then rebuilds the whole comparison in H26's own order — one figure of ours for each figure of theirs (Figs. 5–12, Table 5) — for readers who want to check the reproduction panel by panel rather than argument by argument.

2Data and reduction

All data are public ESO archive holdings. For CD-35 2722 B we use the 18 archival CRIRES+ H1567 epochs (2023 Oct – 2025 Jan; ESO programmes 110.23RW, 111.24M0). Every epoch was re-reduced from the raw frames with the same pipeline version as H26 (cr2res 1.6.10; cal_dark → cal_flat → cal_wave → obs_nodding), keeping the two nodding positions as separate spectra — the extraction H26 favour. Our from-raw reduction reproduces ESO's archived combined products to 42 m s−1 in the final RV. For η Tel B (§6) the same cascade was run on all 20 public H1567 epochs (2023 May – 2025 Aug; programmes 111.24M0, 113.268Y, 115.287U).

3Radial-velocity extraction

RVs are measured with viper in its gas-cell-free CRIRES+ mode, forward-modelling each spectral order against a telluric-free stellar template built from the observations themselves. H26 describe their configuration qualitatively; Table 1 lists their stated choices beside the configuration adopted here and how each element was fixed. Three elements deserve note. The eleven spectral orders were recovered by mapping the paper's order labels through a pre-2024 change in viper's CRIRES+ order numbering, and independently confirmed by the paper's own selection criterion (telluric line density) and by head-to-head scoring. The stellar template requires two build iterations, as H26 state; we find the second iteration is decisive — without it the blind detection of §4 fails its robustness test. Template oversampling and the instrumental-profile model are unstated in H26; a sweep fixes oversampling at 2 and shows the IP choice to be degenerate at this signal-to-noise.

3.1 Validation by injection, not by fit statistics

Self-built templates can absorb the very signal being measured; on the known binary GJ 229 B, one template-iteration recipe recovered only 41% of a known amplitude. Every configuration adopted here was therefore required to transmit an injected Keplerian — imposed by shifting the template per epoch, never the observation, and re-running the full fit — at both K = 1530 and an amplitude-matched K ≈ 306 m s−1. The final CD-35 configuration recovers 105 ± 4% (all eleven orders individually at 92–112%), with the core orders linear at the matched amplitude to 2–8%. The final η Tel B configuration recovers 99–101 ± 1%.

4CD-35 2722 B: the companion reproduces

Figure 1 shows our binned per-nodding series against the published table: 17 usable archival nights match, with rms difference 90 m s−1 (mean order combine; 70–76 with per-order centering and robust combines) against the published per-epoch precision of 57.7. One archival night (BJD 2460604.8) is excluded by an internal quality screen — its across-order scatter is 7× the median night's — and is, independently, the one archival epoch absent from the published table. Figure 2 folds both series on the published period.

The conclusion-level test is a blind period search (ΔBIC of a circular Keplerian over a constant, 4000 trial periods, 5–460 d) on our series alone (Fig. 3, left). The published period family emerges as the top peak (P = 169–171 d, ΔBIC = +40), and — the decisive property — survives the addition of a BERV nuisance covariate (ΔBIC = +25 to +28, rank 1 in mean, median, and clipped combines, on both extraction routes). This matters because the archival sampling correlates orbital phase with the barycentric correction at r = −0.71: with a poorer extraction the same test previously destroyed the detection entirely. Without the second template iteration it still does — per-nodding extraction alone does not rescue it — identifying the template as the decisive systematic.

{{FIG3}}

Two quantitative disagreements persist. Our semi-amplitude runs high, and by how much depends on the estimator. Regressing our RVs on theirs gives a slope of 1.19–1.34 ± 0.10 across the six order combines (+19 to +34%); fitting K directly at the published period gives K = 426–472 m s−1 against the published 306, i.e. +39 to +54%. The two disagree in the expected direction and for the expected reason: the reference series carries its own errors, so the regression slope is attenuated by errors-in-variables while the direct fit is not. We quote both and claim neither. The same phase–BERV degeneracy that once hid the signal also prevents a clean amplitude measurement from these epochs — controlling for BERV absorbs real signal (slope → 1.16 ± 0.12) — so we record the overshoot as a hint only. It is at least consistent in direction with a measured property of the template recipe: on GJ 229 B, self-templating absorbs amplitude, and a recipe that absorbs less would read systematically higher than one that absorbs more. Second, our per-epoch precision remains 1.2–1.6× the published value (Table 2); the residual is night-to-night per-order zero-point scatter that no smooth nuisance model we tried removes without damaging the signal.

4.1 Three tests the detection had not been given

A ΔBIC quoted as the maximum over 4000 trial periods is not a significance: the BIC penalty charges for parameters, not for the size of the search. We therefore calibrate it against the sampling itself. Holding the epoch times, the BERV column and the value distribution fixed, and shuffling the residuals of the base model — signal-free by construction, with the true window function preserved — 2000 realisations give a false-alarm probability of 5×10−4 for the plain search and 2.5×10−3 to 5.5×10−3 once the BERV covariate is included, in every combine. The same null delivers a number worth carrying elsewhere in this field: a signal-free series with this cadence reaches ΔBIC ≈ 19 as its best peak 5% of the time, so peaks in the teens are not evidence — a bar we apply to our own η Tel B residual structure in §6.

Second, no single night carries the result. Dropping each epoch in turn, all 17 leave-one-out subsets return the peak within 6% of 171 d, at ΔBIC +38.5 to +45.7 without the BERV term and +23.9 to +29.8 with it. The largest single-epoch effect is to raise the significance.

Third — and this is the test that speaks to the phase–BERV degeneracy most directly — the period is not a property of the observing cadence. If ~171 d arose from the seasonal structure that CRIRES+ companion programmes share, it should recur in other targets reduced identically. It does not. Pushing every companion series in this project through the same recipe (Table 2a), η Tel B — the same H1567 setting, the same eleven orders, the same pipeline, overlapping seasons and a longer baseline — carries ΔBIC = −3.1 at 171 d, and β Pic b returns −1.8 and −1.7 on two independent reductions. The signal is a property of CD-35 2722 B, not of the calendar.

5The second companion does not survive its own table

H26 report lnZ = −144.32 ± 0.70 for a one-companion (eccentric) model and −141.70 ± 0.69 for two companions: ΔlnZ = +2.62 ± ~1.0 in favour of two. Fitting the same published 23-epoch table with nested sampling (dynesty; likelihood identical to our fitter, priors symmetric between models), every configuration we tried returns the opposite sign (Table 3): pinning the periods at the published values, freeing them in the paper's own windows, or matching the paper's literal both-eccentric pairing; under uniform, linear-jitter, or log-amplitude priors. Across 82 independent sampler runs, 81 land below zero; the single exception reaches +0.90, still far short of the claimed +2.62. The 87.3 d period choice does reproduce — it beats the 70/115 d aliases in both table versions, as in H26 — but the existence evidence does not. Notably, the same comparison run on the superseded preprint table still favours two companions (+3.0): the flip lives in the authors' own data revision (three added epochs, corrected timestamps, near-doubled errors), not in the fitting code. Evidence values are prior-dependent and EMPEROR's exact priors are not published in full; we would welcome the comparison under their exact configuration.

5.1 What a published ΔlnZ uncertainty does and does not mean

Table 3 quotes two uncertainties per row because they differ by up to an order of magnitude, and only one of them is the quantity a reader needs. dynesty's internal estimate — the number a single run reports, and the analogue of what published evidence comparisons normally quote — sits at ±0.24–0.27 in every row here. The measured run-to-run scatter of the same comparison, same data, same priors, differing only in the random seed, is 0.25, 0.62, 0.87, 0.96, 1.19, 1.40 and 2.18 depending on configuration: understated by factors of 1.1 to 8.1.

This is not a convergence problem that more live points repair. Raising nlive fourfold shrinks the internal estimate to ±0.12–0.13, as its N−1/2 scaling demands, while the empirical scatter stays at 0.49–0.62 — the understatement gets worse, reaching 4–5×. The internal number tracks the sampler's own accounting of its integration, not the dispersion of answers the procedure actually returns.

The consequence for the disagreement in this section is worth stating plainly, because it is more collegial than it first sounds. H26's ΔlnZ = +2.62 rests on lnZ uncertainties of ±0.70 and ±0.69, which are of the same class and, on this evidence, likely to be understated by a similar factor. If the reproducibility of their comparison resembles what we measure for ours, +2.62 is not significant on its own terms — before any argument about priors, and consistent with their own description of the second companion as tentative. We cannot test their sampler and do not claim to have. The falsifiable version of this is a question they can answer in an afternoon: how many independent runs stand behind +2.62, and what is the scatter among them? We would publish that number gladly, whichever way it falls.

6η Tel B: a first radial-velocity limit, below a Jupiter mass

η Tel B (M7.5, ~47 MJup, β Pic moving group) shares CD-35 2722 B's instrument setting and has no RV measurements in the literature. Before any extraction we checked the property that compromised the CD-35 sampling: over the 17 usable modern epochs, only 11% of the 5–460 d period grid is BERV-degenerate, and the 150–300 d decade is fully clean — this target cannot hide an orbit behind the barycentric correction. The transferred pipeline delivers 127–129 m s−1 per epoch (the M7.5 spectrum is far richer in H-band lines than an early-L), r(RV, BERV) = +0.03, and injection recovery of 99–101 ± 1% with per-epoch injection residuals of only 12–26 m s−1.

The blind search returns no credible signal on either extraction route (Fig. 3, right): the whole BERV-clean window is negative, and the only structure — a weak comb below 20 d whose leading period moves between routes and combines, and which strengthens only when the BERV term is added — behaves as a sampling alias, in the same period regime H26 themselves discard as artifacts. The null is calibrated end to end: a real K = 300 m s−1, P = 200 d Keplerian, injected at the template level and pushed through the full pipeline, is recovered by the same search at rank 1 (ΔBIC +15 to +19). Marginalizing injected phase and demanding ΔBIC ≥ 10 with rank 1 at the injected period gives the 90% exclusion of Figure 4 and Table 4: companions of m sin i ≳ 0.5–1.2 MJup are ruled out across P = 20–300 d. A twin of the CD-35 companion would have been detected with ~70% probability.

7Discussion

Three conclusions. First, the central claim of H26 stands up to a from-raw, independently configured, injection-validated reproduction: CD-35 2722 B's RVs contain a coherent ~170-day signal that a blind search finds at high significance and that survives the BERV degeneracy of the sampling. Second, the second companion does not: on the paper's own revised table the evidence integral lands on the other side of zero in every prior family we tried, and the claimed +2.62 is itself within ~2.7σ of zero by the paper's own quoted lnZ uncertainties. Third, the method transfers: on its first new target it produced a sub-Jupiter companion limit from archival data alone.

A metrological note. The paper's per-epoch precision statistic (its Eq. 1, an error-weighted across-order dispersion) moved against our two largest real improvements while the external agreement improved by 40%: it is invariant to a common-mode signal by construction, and it does not measure night-level accuracy. In a regime where every internal statistic we tried was, at least once, anti-correlated with truth, injection recovery plus an external reference series were the only reliable guides. Reproductions of this class of measurement should assume the same.

The open items are dated. The amplitude excess and the second-companion discrepancy both become decidable when the embargoed 2026 epochs release (Dec 2026 – May 2027), which break the phase–BERV degeneracy; and the η Tel B limit can be extended by the 2009 pre-upgrade CRIRES epochs (a 16-year baseline) and by the K-band epochs still under embargo. The natural next targets, in order of readiness: β Pic b (six archival nights; a first RV series of the planet, not yet an orbit), and PDS 70 b, where ~200–300 M⊕ exomoon-regime companions would be within reach of a dedicated campaign.

8.1 The detection itself (their Figs. 1, 2, 7)

The primary result reproduces in the plainest possible terms. Run the same generalised Lomb-Scargle on their published table and on our from-raw series and the two periodograms are near-twins (Fig. 5, top row): their 23 epochs peak at 171.0 d with power 0.85, our 17 nights at 170.3 d with power 0.93, both past the 0.1% false-alarm level, both with the same ~365 d alias shoulder. On the model-residual rows the two series part company in an informative way. After removing one satellite, their residuals peak at 87.2 d — this is the second satellite, visible exactly where their Fig. 1 puts it — while ours peak at 44.3 d, and neither peak clears 1%.

{{FIG5}} {{FIG6}}

Their Fig. 7 is the paper's systematics defence: GLS periodograms of every viper output, showing that no instrumental or atmospheric parameter repeats the RV periodicity. It reproduces (Fig. 10) — across ten fitted quantities the largest power at 171 d is 0.42, from the linear wavelength-solution coefficient, and most sit below 0.2. But the panel that matters most is one their figure does not contain. The barycentric correction is not a viper output, so it is not in their grid; put it there and it carries power 0.66 at the signal period, three times the typical instrumental term. That is the phase–BERV degeneracy of §4 seen directly, and it is why our detection is quoted with a BERV nuisance covariate rather than without one.

8.2 The extraction choice (their Fig. 4)

H26's Fig. 4 makes one measurement: how much is gained by computing RVs for the two nodding positions separately and binning, instead of combining the spectra first. Their published answer is 4.9% (60.50 → 57.68 m s−1 mean internal error); the preprint's was ~10%, on a different error scale. Our answer depends on the order combine, which theirs cannot show because a single number hides it: paired on five nights at identical configuration the per-nodding route is 21% better, and across all 17 matched nights it is 18% better with a robust combine — but 6% worse with a plain mean (Fig. 7). The direction they report is real and if anything understated; the magnitude is not a property of the nodding choice alone.

8.3 The second satellite (their Figs. 5, 6, 8, 9)

Here the comparison is at its sharpest, because the parameters reproduce and the conclusion does not. Their Fig. 5 shows the second signal's period posterior falling into four peaks — ~14, 70, 88 and 115 d — and argues the 88 d one is the real solution. Give the same table a wide P2 prior and three of those four peaks come back unprompted (Fig. 8, top): 70, 88 and 115 d, with 14 d essentially unpopulated. Fit each window separately and the 88 d window is the best of the four, exactly as they conclude. Their Fig. 6 makes the case against the 14 d solution visually; ours does too (Fig. 9) — the 14 d model is the same implausible high-frequency comb through the same points.

What does not follow is the existence claim. Every one of those four windows is worse than a single eccentric satellite on the same table under the same priors: ΔlnZ = −2.8 at 88 d, and −4.9, −5.6, −6.2 at the others. The best-supported second satellite available in this data still loses to no second satellite at all. The corner plots say the same thing in parameter space. For the large satellite (Fig. 11) their Table 1 values sit comfortably inside our posterior — P = 171.6+1.0−1.2 d against their 171.11, m sin i = 0.96 ± 0.1 MJup against their 0.918, with only eccentricity marginally high. For the small satellite (Fig. 12) the same fit that recovers their period to 90.4+5.4−8.0 d leaves its amplitude unconstrained: K2 peaks at zero, and m sin i = 0.07+0.08−0.05 MJup puts their 0.219 in the tail. A period can be well determined for a signal whose amplitude is consistent with nothing being there; that is the whole disagreement.

8.4 Stellar contamination without the slit viewer (their Fig. 3)

H26's Fig. 3 fits the primary's PSF in the slit-viewer frames and converts it to a contamination fraction at the companion's position, worst night ~15%. We have never reduced the acquisition imaging, but the nodding products carry the same information along a different axis. The extracted swath spans the full slit — the trace-wave order height is 179.8 px at 0.056″/px, i.e. 10.07″, sampled by a 512-point slit function at 0.0197″ per point — and the slit position angle is pinned at POSANG = 153.1° on all eighteen nights with a 6″ nod throw. The primary therefore falls at a fixed offset from the companion trace, on one side or the other, in every frame of the campaign. Rather than assume that offset we scan it: each profile is searched over 1.5–4.5″ on both sides, which brackets both the 2.8″ projected separation this work adopts and the 3.17″ value in the literature.

No primary peak is detected. At the adopted 2.8″ the slit-function height relative to the companion's peak has a median of 0.0014 against a local profile noise of 0.0057 — 0.25σ. The corresponding 3σ bound is 1.7% of the companion's peak, well inside H26's ~15% worst-night estimate. Two internal checks say the non-detection is not an artefact of where we looked. First, the fraction of profiles exceeding 3σ is the same at 2.8″ (3.6%) and at 3.17″ (3.1%) — no localised excess singles out either separation. Second, across the full scan the strongest feature sits at a median of 1.75″, at the inner edge of the search: that is the companion's own PSF skirt, and 23.8% of profiles exceed 3σ somewhere in the scan purely because a wide search offers many chances. The geometry predicts a non-detection anyway, since a primary several arcseconds off-axis along a slit only 0.2″ wide contributes no core flux.

Two caveats belong with the number. The profile median is removed before measurement, so this bounds a resolved second trace and not a smooth halo pedestal — it complements the slit-viewer method rather than replacing it. And the one epoch our internal screen rejects, BJD 2460604.81, is not explained by contamination: it sits within the scatter of the other seventeen and was taken on the campaign's best seeing (image-analysis FWHM 0.60″). We record it as unexplained.

Verification is primarily mechanical rather than expert-audited: every adopted pipeline change was scored against the published RV series only and required signal-injection recovery (template shifted, never the observation), positive controls preceded any null, and the blind search carried a BERV nuisance covariate; dead ends and retractions remain in the public record. Known residual confounds (the RV–BERV correlation of the published series; the 20–40% amplitude excess) are reported in the text rather than resolved by assumption. No submission or correspondence is automated. This work uses only public archival observations and involves no human or animal subjects. A stage-by-stage AI-involvement checklist with evidence pointers, including per-stage model versions via the commit trail, is maintained at github.com/mepotts/astronomy/AI-CHECKLIST.md.

Data & code availability

All spectra are public ESO archive products and raw frames (programmes 110.23RW, 111.24M0, 113.268Y, 115.287U, 083.C-0759, 383.D-0183). The full pipeline — reduction drivers, the converter, injection harness, nested-sampling code, and every script behind the figures and tables — lives in the project repository (exosat-rv/, milestones M0–M18). The published RV table is transcribed from the H26 appendix with provenance headers.
