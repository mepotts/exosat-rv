A from‑raw, paper‑calibrated reanalysis of the CD‑35 2722 B exosatellite, and a re‑examination of the evidence for a second companion

Matthew Potts · unaffiliated analysis

11 August 2026 · figure-by-figure appendix added 12 August 2026 · audit revision 31 August 2026 · reanalyses Hoy et al. (2026), Nature · all data from the public ESO archive

Abstract

Hoy et al. (2026) report the radial-velocity detection of a ~0.9 MJup companion to the young brown dwarf CD-35 2722 B, with tentative evidence for a second, smaller companion. Starting from the raw CRIRES+ frames in the ESO archive, we use a separately implemented extraction (cr2res 1.6.10 + viper) whose orders, template iteration and other configuration choices were calibrated with the published RV series visible. A downstream period search whose fits do not ingest the published RV values, but whose historical driver is target- and paper-aware, recovers P ≈ 169–171 d as the top peak on the 17 nights retained by an internal across-order-spread screen (ΔBIC +40), and the peak survives a barycentric-correction nuisance term (ΔBIC +25 to +28). With all 18 archival nights, however, the search degrades to noise; the quality exclusion is objective but load-bearing, and the 17/17 leave-one-out result is conditional on it. A 5000-permutation null with BERV included gives a nominal global p of about 0.002–0.008; without BERV the range is about 0.0002–0.0006. These values assume exchangeability of the fitted base-model residuals and are conditional on the post-hoc screen, so they are not confirmatory false-alarm probabilities. The same-setting η Tel B control has no 171 d power, arguing against a shared observing-calendar origin. Our per-epoch precision reaches 70–90 m s−1 against the published 57.7, and our semi-amplitude runs high by +19 to +34% (regression slope) or +39 to +54% (direct fit), which we do not interpret. On the published Nature RV table, the two-companion model is not favoured under any model/prior configuration tested here: 81 of 82 paired evidence comparisons are negative. This establishes non-reproduction under our stated likelihoods and priors; our dynesty seed scatter does not measure the reproducibility of the authors' different sampler.

1Introduction

Companion-side radial velocities — measuring the reflex motion of a directly imaged brown dwarf or giant planet from its own spectrum — were proposed by Vanderburg et al. (2018), with the detectability framework for imaged companions set out by Lazzoni et al. (2022). The radial-velocity forecast is Ruffio et al. (2023), who applied it to HR 7672 B and projected sensitivity for fibre-fed high-resolution spectrographs — KPIC now, HISPEC and TMT/MODHIS later — rather than for CRIRES+. Four published searches have returned nulls — the HR 8799 planets (Vanderburg & Rodriguez 2021), HR 7672 B (Ruffio et al. 2023), GQ Lup B (Horstman et al. 2024) and, most recently and on this same instrument, β Pictoris b at 160 m s−1 mean precision (Kenworthy et al. 2026) — before Hoy et al. (2026, hereafter H26) reported a detection: a P = 171.1 d, K ≈ 306 m s−1 signal in the L0-type companion CD-35 2722 B (31–37 MJup, AB Dor moving group), interpreted as a 0.92 MJup satellite, with tentative evidence (ΔlnZ = +2.62) for a second companion at 87.3 d.

The technique occupies a specific and defensible niche. Astrometric monitoring of the companion — from interferometry (Macias et al. 2026; Kral et al. 2026) or from high-contrast imaging (Lazzoni et al. 2026) — probes the same reflex motion, but the two scale oppositely with the satellite's orbit: astrometric amplitude grows with semi-major axis a while radial-velocity amplitude falls as a−1/2 (Macias et al. 2026). Radial velocity therefore owns the close orbits and astrometry the wide ones, and the division is sharp in practice. The satellite at issue here sits at roughly 0.2 au from its host, while the dedicated SPHERE star-hopping survey of twelve directly imaged companions constrains satellites only beyond ~1–5 au (Lazzoni et al. 2026). There is no overlap, and no imaging programme is expected to close the gap — which is why a radial-velocity detection in this regime is worth testing carefully rather than taking on trust.

A detection of this kind is worth reanalysing from raw data: the claimed amplitude sits far below the per-spectrum noise, the epoch sampling entangles the orbital phase with the barycentric correction (§4), and the published evidence for the second companion moved substantially between the preprint and the accepted version. This draft reports (i) a from-raw, separately implemented but paper-calibrated extraction of the H26 measurement (§2–4, Figs. 1–3), (ii) a re-examination of the two-companion evidence on the published RV table (§5, Table 3). Fitter-stage velocity transmission is tested by signal injection rather than by internal fit statistics; the distinction turns out to matter (§6). §7 then rebuilds the whole comparison in H26's own order — one figure of ours for each figure of theirs (Figs. 4–11, Table 4) — for readers who want to check the comparison panel by panel rather than argument by argument.

2Data and reduction

All data are public ESO archive holdings. For CD-35 2722 B we use the 18 archival CRIRES+ H1567 epochs (2023 Oct – 2025 Jan; ESO programmes 110.23RW, 111.24M0). Every epoch was re-reduced from the raw frames with the same pipeline version as H26 (cr2res 1.6.10; cal_dark → cal_flat → cal_wave → obs_nodding), keeping the two nodding positions as separate spectra — the extraction H26 favour. Our from-raw reduction matches ESO's archived combined products to 42 m s−1 in the final RV. The same cascade was run on all 20 public H1567 epochs of η Tel B (2023 May – 2025 Aug; programmes 111.24M0, 113.268Y, 115.287U), which serve here as the common-mode control of §5 — the same instrument setting and a longer baseline, so a period intrinsic to the observing calendar rather than to CD-35 2722 B would appear in both. Those data also support a radial-velocity limit on η Tel B itself, reported separately.

3Radial-velocity extraction

RVs are measured with viper in its gas-cell-free CRIRES+ mode, forward-modelling each spectral order against a telluric-free stellar template built from the observations themselves. H26 describe their configuration qualitatively; Table 1 lists their stated choices beside the configuration adopted here and how each element was fixed. Three elements deserve note. The eleven spectral orders were recovered by mapping the paper's order labels through a pre-2024 change in viper's CRIRES+ order numbering, and separately cross-checked against the paper's own selection criterion (telluric line density) and by head-to-head scoring. The stellar template requires two build iterations, as H26 state; we find the second iteration is decisive — without it the screened period result of §4 fails its robustness test. Template oversampling and the instrumental-profile model are unstated in H26; a sweep fixes oversampling at 2 and shows the IP choice to be degenerate at this signal-to-noise.

3.1 Fitter-stage validation by injection, not by fit statistics

Self-built templates can absorb the very signal being measured; on the known binary GJ 229 B, one template-iteration recipe recovered only 41% of a known amplitude. Every configuration adopted here was therefore required to transmit an injected Keplerian — imposed by shifting the already-built template per epoch, never the observation, and re-running the RV fit — at both K = 1530 and an amplitude-matched K ≈ 306 m s−1. The final CD-35 configuration recovers 105 ± 4% (all eleven orders individually at 92–112%), with the core orders linear at the matched amplitude to 2–8%. The final η Tel B configuration recovers 99–101 ± 1%.

This gate begins after template construction. It answers “given this template, does the fitter transmit velocity?”, not “would constructing the template from data that contain a real orbit absorb part of that orbit?”. The external comparison to the published CD-35 series bounds that second failure mode only for CD-35; η Tel B has no equivalent external check. These injections therefore validate fitter-stage transmission, not the full self-templating pipeline.

4CD-35 2722 B: the screened series recovers the signal

Figure 1 shows our binned per-nodding series against the published table: 17 usable archival nights match, with rms difference 90 m s−1 (mean order combine; 70–76 with per-order centering and robust combines) against the published per-epoch precision of 57.7. One archival night (BJD 2460604.8) is excluded by an internal quality screen — its across-order scatter is 7× the median night's — and also happens to be the one archival epoch absent from the published table. This exclusion is load-bearing: with all 18 nights included, the period search degrades to noise. The screen identifies an objectively extreme epoch without using the published RV values, but the detection and every subsequent robustness statistic in this section are conditional on the 17-night screened set. Figure 2 folds both retained series on the published period.

The conclusion-level test is a downstream period search (ΔBIC of a circular Keplerian over a constant, 4000 trial periods, 5–460 d) on our screened series alone. The fits do not ingest the published RV values, but the historical driver imports the published table to construct the matched subset and reports a hard-coded 171.45-day comparison window; we therefore describe it as target- and paper-aware, not blind (Fig. 3, left). The published period family emerges as the top peak (P = 169–171 d, ΔBIC = +40), and — the decisive property — survives the addition of a BERV nuisance covariate (ΔBIC = +25 to +28, rank 1 in mean, median, and clipped combines, on both extraction routes). This matters because the archival sampling correlates orbital phase with the barycentric correction at r = −0.71: with a poorer extraction the same test previously destroyed the detection entirely. Without the second template iteration it still does — per-nodding extraction alone does not rescue it — identifying the template as the decisive systematic.

Two quantitative disagreements persist. Our semi-amplitude runs high, and by how much depends on the estimator. Regressing our RVs on theirs gives a slope of 1.19–1.34 ± 0.10 across the six order combines (+19 to +34%); fitting K directly at the published period gives K = 426–472 m s−1 against the published 306, i.e. +39 to +54%. The two disagree in the expected direction and for the expected reason: the reference series carries its own errors, so the regression slope is attenuated by errors-in-variables while the direct fit is not. We quote both and claim neither. The same phase–BERV degeneracy that once hid the signal also prevents a clean amplitude measurement from these epochs — controlling for BERV absorbs real signal (slope → 1.16 ± 0.12) — so we record the overshoot as a hint only. It is at least consistent in direction with a measured property of the template recipe: on GJ 229 B, self-templating absorbs amplitude, and a recipe that absorbs less would read systematically higher than one that absorbs more. Second, our per-epoch precision remains 1.2–1.6× the published value (Table 2); the residual is night-to-night per-order zero-point scatter that no smooth nuisance model we tried removes without damaging the signal.

4.1 Three tests the detection had not been given

A ΔBIC quoted as the maximum over 4000 trial periods is not a significance: the BIC penalty charges for parameters, not for the size of the search. We therefore calibrate it against the sampling itself. Holding the epoch times, the BERV column and the value distribution fixed, and shuffling the residuals of the base model — signal-free by construction, with the true window function preserved — 5000 realisations give nominal global permutation p-values of 0.0002–0.0006 for the plain search and 0.0022–0.0076 once the BERV covariate is included, across mean, median and clipped combines. They are conditional on exchangeability of the fitted base-model residuals and on a post-hoc quality screen, so they are diagnostic rather than confirmatory false-alarm probabilities. On all 18 nights the corresponding nominal BERV-adjusted values are 0.31–0.91, quantifying the screen's leverage. Under the same conditional null, a signal-free series with this cadence reaches ΔBIC ≈ 19 as its best peak 5% of the time, so peaks in the teens are not evidence — a bar we apply to our own η Tel B control series in Table 2a as strictly as to H26.

Second, within the already-screened sample, no one of the remaining nights carries the result. Dropping each retained epoch in turn, all 17 leave-one-out subsets return the peak within 6% of 171 d, at ΔBIC +38.5 to +45.7 without the BERV term and +23.9 to +29.8 with it. The largest single-epoch effect is to raise the test statistic.

Third — and this is the test that speaks to the phase–BERV degeneracy most directly — the control data argue against a period set only by the observing cadence. If ~171 d arose from the seasonal structure that CRIRES+ companion programmes share, it should recur in other targets reduced with the same setting and cadence. It does not. Applying the same search recipe (Table 2a), η Tel B — the same H1567 setting, the same eleven orders, the same pipeline, overlapping seasons and a longer baseline — carries ΔBIC = −3.1 at 171 d, and β Pic b returns −1.8 and −1.7 on two K-band slit reductions, although those unresolved, host-dominated spectra are not a same-setting control. The η Tel result is the clean comparison and argues against, but cannot by itself prove the absence of, a shared calendar systematic.

4.2 Host-star photometry and Gaia catalogue context

A nightly and per-camera ASAS-SN reanalysis finds no coherent host-star modulation at 171.454 d (nominal night-permutation p = 0.13–0.16 across the host/filter rows, conditional on exchangeability of the final camera-corrected night bins). The two source IDs are alternative aperture photometry of the same 2,173 timestamp/camera measurements, not independent replications. On nested deterministic grids of 720, 1440 and 2880 phases, the four rows first reach at least 90% phase recovery at K = 12/13/12/13 mmag; the grid-resolved cross-series threshold is therefore 13 mmag semiamplitude (26 mmag peak to peak). The previously quoted 5 mmag signal is recovered in only 43.2–44.2% of the finest-grid phases, and the maximum successive-grid fraction change over the curves is <0.0007. These are numerical uniform-phase fractions conditional on one observed-noise realization, the preprocessing and an estimated fixed-period permutation threshold, not binomial confidence bounds. This is a photometric null at that conditional sensitivity, not proof that the RV signal is nonstellar.

Gaia DR3 gives the primary RUWE = 1.023 and no non-single-star solution. These are useful catalogue checks, but they do not establish that the host is astrometrically unperturbed: the reported astrometric excess noise is 0.099 mas and formally significant, and RUWE/NSS alone cannot exclude an unresolved companion. Neither external check is used to raise the RV detection significance.

5The second companion is not reproduced under the tested models

H26 report lnZ = −144.32 ± 0.70 for a one-companion (eccentric) model and −141.70 ± 0.69 for two companions: ΔlnZ = +2.62 ± ~1.0 in favour of two. Fitting the same published 23-epoch table with nested sampling (dynesty; likelihood identical to our fitter, priors symmetric between models), every configuration mean we obtain has the opposite sign (Table 3): pinning the periods at the published values, freeing them in the paper's own windows, or matching the paper's literal both-eccentric pairing; under uniform, linear-jitter, or log-amplitude priors. Across 82 paired model-evidence comparisons (164 nested-sampler invocations), 81 land below zero; the single exception reaches +0.90, still far short of the claimed +2.62. The 87.3 d period choice does reproduce — it beats the 70/115 d aliases in both table versions, as in H26 — but the existence evidence does not. Notably, the same comparison run on the superseded preprint table still favours two companions (+3.0): the flip lives in the authors' own data revision (three added epochs, corrected timestamps, near-doubled errors), not in the fitting code. Evidence values are prior-dependent and EMPEROR's exact priors are not published in full; we would welcome the comparison under their exact configuration.

5.1 What a published ΔlnZ uncertainty does and does not mean

Table 3 quotes two uncertainties per row because they differ by up to an order of magnitude and answer different questions. dynesty's internal estimate — the number a single run reports, and the analogue of what published evidence comparisons normally quote — sits at ±0.24–0.27 in the nlive = 500 rows. The measured run-to-run scatter of the same comparison, same data, same priors, differing only in the random seed, is 0.25, 0.62, 0.87, 0.96, 1.19, 1.40 and 2.18 depending on configuration: understated by factors of 1.1 to 8.1.

Increasing nlive is useful but does not make the internal estimate interchangeable with empirical reproducibility. At four times the live points, one partly unconverged row's scatter tightens substantially (2.18 to 0.54) and its mean moves from −5.51 to −3.49; the other high-nlive rows have scatter 0.49–0.62. The internal estimates nevertheless shrink to ±0.12–0.13, so all three high-nlive ratios remain 4–5×. The convergence of a mean and the seed-to-seed reproducibility of a comparison are separate diagnostics.

The consequence for the disagreement in this section is worth stating plainly, because it is more collegial than it first sounds. H26's ΔlnZ = +2.62 rests on lnZ uncertainties of ±0.70 and ±0.69, which are internal estimates of a related kind. Our dynesty experiment shows why repeated-run scatter is worth asking for, but it does not establish the scatter of H26's EMPEROR calculation or change the significance of their value. Their exact sampler behaviour and incompletely published priors require their exact configuration. The falsifiable question is therefore: how many independent comparisons stand behind +2.62, and what is the scatter among them? Until that is known, our defensible conclusion is only non-reproduction under the models and priors tested here, consistent with their description of the second companion as tentative.

5.2 A separate implementation with paper-aware calibration

The pipeline used here is not a reconstruction of H26's procedure, which has not been published; it is a separately implemented route from raw frames to velocities. Its configuration (Table 1), however, was selected by minimising rms against the published CD-35 RV series, with every adopted choice additionally required to transmit an injected Keplerian at the fitter stage. That is calibration with the target answer visible, not an end-to-end independent reproduction.

The strongest uncontaminated transfer test is narrower: the same H1567 nodding recipe on η Tel B returns 99–101% fitter-stage injection recovery and a null over a longer baseline. Other valid nodding datasets use setting- or target-specific recipes. Every dataset originally classified here as staring mode was later identified as HiRISE fibre data that had been processed through the wrong slit recipe; those scientific verdicts were withdrawn. We therefore do not claim unmodified transfer to nine companions or validation across both observing modes.

The period-search fits use no published RV values, and the internal spread screen selects the one epoch absent from H26's table. The surrounding machinery is nevertheless target- and paper-aware: the driver imports the published table to construct its matched subset and hard-codes a 171.45-day reporting window. Neither the downstream search nor the extraction choices, quality-screen development or analysis as a whole is a blind reproduction. The screen is also load-bearing because the all-18-night series has no robust detection.

We can bound what that buys. Twenty-one complete RV series exist from these same spectra under different configurations, and scoring each for both rms against the published series and ΔBIC near 171 d separates the two questions. Across configurations spanning a factor 2.1 in rms agreement, five of the ten configurations that match the published series worse than median still detect the period at ΔBIC > 10, and the correlation between the tuning metric and the detection is only −0.31. Some association is expected whether or not the signal is real, since a better extraction both agrees better with any competent measurement of the same photons and recovers a real signal more strongly; what would indicate circularity is the signal vanishing away from the best-matching configurations, and it does not. In degraded configurations the period weakens and loses rank before it disappears, behaviour consistent with a signal that weakens with extraction quality. This does not prove the signal is physical or establish what would have happened had the family itself been chosen without the published series.

This bounds selection within a configuration family that was itself explored with the published series available; it does not bound the family's boundaries. M36 attempted an injection-selected holdout experiment, but its design was itself paper-derived: its injection plan fixed K = 1530 m s−1 at the published 171.454-day orbit. Its execution was also invalid/inconclusive: the gate ignored recovery-slope uncertainty, the runner did not pass the pre-registered polynomial degrees, and the scorer could compare means formed from different valid-order sets. A valid replacement requires a new frozen protocol, fresh validation material and new extraction runs. End-to-end independence therefore remains open.

6Discussion

Two qualified conclusions. First, a separately implemented, paper-calibrated from-raw extraction recovers a coherent ~170-day signal on the 17 nights retained by an objective but load-bearing quality screen. A target- and paper-aware downstream search whose fits do not ingest the published RV values survives the BERV nuisance term, with a nominal global permutation p of about 0.002–0.008 under residual exchangeability and the post-hoc screen; the all-18-night series gives 0.31–0.91. This is diagnostic null calibration, not a confirmatory false-alarm probability. Second, the two-companion model is not favoured on the paper's revised table under any model/prior configuration tested here. This does not measure the behaviour of H26's different sampler. Signal injection validates fitter-stage transmission after template construction, not every stage of the pipeline.

One piece of context belongs with all three, and it is easy to miss because the relevant number is rarely quoted. CD-35 2722 B is an exceptionally favourable target. Its host is an M1 V star at K = 7.05 and the companion sits at K = 12.01, a difference of 4.96 mag — a flux ratio of just 97:1. Across the archival companions reduced here with the same pipeline that is the easiest by a wide margin: η Tel B sits at ~430:1, PDS 70 at ~460:1, AB Pic b at ~1770:1, and β Pic b — where the companion's spectrum drowns in its host's — at ~3950:1. (Ratios are band-matched in K and derived from magnitudes rather than asserted. The two resting on measured companion photometry are η Tel B, Ks = 11.6 (Neuhäuser et al. 2011), and β Pic b, Ks = 12.47 (Currie et al. 2013); the other two come from a compiled catalogue column that primary sources have contradicted in both cases anyone has checked, and are indicative only.) A low-mass host with a massive brown-dwarf companion is the most forgiving configuration this technique has, and it is where the detection was made.

This does not resolve the signal's physical interpretation: it recovers conditionally in the screened archival series, while end-to-end independence remains open. It also bounds what may be inferred from the favourable CD-35 geometry. Planetary-mass companions around brighter hosts sit two to three orders of magnitude further out in contrast, and this work contains no measured point between ~430:1, where extraction is clean, and ~3950:1, where it fails. Contrast alone does not order these outcomes — PDS 70 b fails at ~460:1 while AB Pic b is clean at ~1770:1 — and what separates them is whether the pair is resolved at the slit at all, which we treat separately. That companion-side radial velocimetry can find a satellite is not yet evidence that it can do so where most directly imaged planets live.

A metrological note. The paper's per-epoch precision statistic (its Eq. 1, an error-weighted across-order dispersion) moved against our two largest real improvements while rms against the published series fell from 133 to 85 m s−1 along the documented adoption chain. The order combine also changed, so this is not a controlled single-change comparison. The statistic is invariant to a common-mode signal by construction, and it does not measure night-level accuracy. In a regime where every internal statistic we tried was, at least once, anti-correlated with truth, injection recovery plus an external reference series were the only reliable guides. Reanalyses of this class of measurement should assume the same.

The open items are dated. The amplitude excess and the second-companion discrepancy both become decidable when the embargoed 2026 epochs release (Dec 2026 – May 2027), which break the phase–BERV degeneracy. Beyond that, the question this paper leaves open is not about CD-35 2722 B at all but about how many such systems carry satellites: β Pic b has since been searched to 1 MJup at P = 200 d without a detection (Kenworthy et al. 2026), while PDS 70 b — where ~200–300 M⊕ exomoon-regime companions would be within reach of a dedicated campaign — has not been searched at all.

8.1 The detection itself (their Figs. 1, 2, 7)

The screened series has closely matching GLS morphology. Run the same generalised Lomb-Scargle on their published table and on our from-raw series and the two periodograms are near-twins (Fig. 5, top row): their 23 epochs peak at 171.0 d with power 0.85, our 17 nights at 170.3 d with power 0.93, both past the plotted 0.1% GLS false-alarm level, both with the same ~365 d alias shoulder. That plotted threshold is distinct from the nominal global permutation p in §4.1, and our 17-night result remains conditional on the load-bearing screen. On the model-residual rows the two series part company in an informative way. After removing one satellite, their residuals peak at 87.2 d — this is the second satellite, visible exactly where their Fig. 1 puts it — while ours peak at 44.3 d, and neither peak clears 1%.

{{FIG5}} {{FIG6}}

Their Fig. 7 is the paper's systematics defence: GLS periodograms of every viper output, showing that no instrumental or atmospheric parameter repeats the RV periodicity. The same diagnostic pattern appears (Fig. 10) — across ten fitted quantities the largest power at 171 d is 0.42, from the linear wavelength-solution coefficient, and most sit below 0.2. But the panel that matters most is one their figure does not contain. The barycentric correction is not a viper output, so it is not in their grid; put it there and it carries power 0.66 at the signal period, three times the typical instrumental term. That is the phase–BERV degeneracy of §4 seen directly, and it is why our detection is quoted with a BERV nuisance covariate rather than without one.

8.2 The extraction choice (their Fig. 4)

H26's Fig. 4 makes one measurement: how much is gained by computing RVs for the two nodding positions separately and binning, instead of combining the spectra first. Their published answer is 4.9% (60.50 → 57.68 m s−1 mean internal error); the preprint's was ~10%, on a different error scale. Our answer depends on the order combine, which theirs cannot show because a single number hides it: paired on five nights at identical configuration the per-nodding route is 21% better, and across all 17 matched nights it is 18% better with a robust combine — but 6% worse with a plain mean (Fig. 7). The direction they report is real and if anything understated; the magnitude is not a property of the nodding choice alone.

8.3 The second satellite (their Figs. 5, 6, 8, 9)

Here the comparison is at its sharpest, because the period structure agrees and the conclusion does not. Their Fig. 5 shows the second signal's period posterior falling into four peaks — ~14, 70, 88 and 115 d — and argues the 88 d one is the real solution. Give the same table a wide P2 prior and three of those four peaks come back unprompted (Fig. 8, top): 70, 88 and 115 d, with 14 d essentially unpopulated. Fit each window separately and the 88 d window is the best of the four, exactly as they conclude. Their Fig. 6 makes the case against the 14 d solution visually; ours does too (Fig. 9) — the 14 d model is the same implausible high-frequency comb through the same points.

What does not follow is the existence claim. Every one of those four windows is worse than a single eccentric satellite on the same table under the same priors: ΔlnZ = −2.8 at 88 d, and −4.9, −5.6, −6.2 at the others. The best-supported second satellite available in this data still loses to no second satellite at all. The corner plots say the same thing in parameter space. For the large satellite (Fig. 11) their Table 1 values sit comfortably inside our posterior — P = 171.6+1.0−1.2 d against their 171.11, m sin i = 0.96 ± 0.1 MJup against their 0.918, with only eccentricity marginally high. For the small satellite (Fig. 12) the same fit that recovers their period to 90.4+5.4−8.0 d leaves its amplitude unconstrained: K2 peaks at zero, and m sin i = 0.07+0.08−0.05 MJup puts their 0.219 in the tail. A period can be well determined for a signal whose amplitude is consistent with nothing being there; that is the whole disagreement.

8.4 Stellar contamination without the slit viewer (their Fig. 3)

H26's Fig. 3 fits the primary's PSF in the slit-viewer frames and converts it to a contamination fraction at the companion's position, worst night ~15%. We have never reduced the acquisition imaging, but the nodding products carry the same information along a different axis. The extracted swath spans the full slit — the trace-wave order height is 179.8 px at 0.056″/px, i.e. 10.07″, sampled by a 512-point slit function at 0.0197″ per point — and the slit position angle is pinned at POSANG = 153.1° on all eighteen nights with a 6″ nod throw. The primary therefore falls at a fixed offset from the companion trace, on one side or the other, in every frame of the campaign. Rather than assume that offset we scan it: each profile is searched over 1.5–4.5″ on both sides, which comfortably brackets the 2.8″ projected separation (H26 quote ~2.8″ in both the preprint and the published version). An earlier pass here sampled a single offset built from 3.17″, a value we can find in no archived source; scanning removes the need to commit to either.

No primary peak is detected. At the adopted 2.8″ the slit-function height relative to the companion's peak has a median of 0.0014 against a local profile noise of 0.0057 — 0.25σ. The corresponding 3σ bound is 1.7% of the companion's peak, well inside H26's ~15% worst-night estimate. Two internal checks say the non-detection is not an artefact of where we looked. First, the fraction of profiles exceeding 3σ is the same at 2.8″ (3.6%) as at 3.17″ (3.1%) — no localised excess singles out either separation. Second, across the full scan the strongest feature sits at a median of 1.75″, at the inner edge of the search: that is the companion's own PSF skirt, and 23.8% of profiles exceed 3σ somewhere in the scan purely because a wide search offers many chances. The geometry predicts a non-detection anyway, since a primary several arcseconds off-axis along a slit only 0.2″ wide contributes no core flux.

Two caveats belong with the number. The profile median is removed before measurement, so this bounds a resolved second trace and not a smooth halo pedestal — it complements the slit-viewer method rather than replacing it. And the one epoch our internal screen rejects, BJD 2460604.81, is not explained by contamination: it sits within the scatter of the other seventeen and was taken on the campaign's best seeing (image-analysis FWHM 0.60″). We record it as unexplained.

Verification is primarily mechanical rather than expert-audited: every CD-35 extraction change adopted into the central recipe was scored against the published RV series and required fitter-stage signal-injection recovery (an already-built template shifted, never the observation), valid nulls were conditioned on positive controls, and the downstream period search carried a BERV nuisance covariate; dead ends and retractions remain in the public record. Known residual confounds (the RV–BERV correlation of the published series; the amplitude excess, +19–34% by regression slope and +39–54% by direct fit) are reported in the text rather than resolved by assumption. No submission or correspondence is automated. This work uses only public archival observations and involves no human or animal subjects.
